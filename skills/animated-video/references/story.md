# Directing the story

Use this whenever you have to invent or shape the story. A polished animation cannot rescue a
weak story, and the story is the cheapest thing to change: fix it in text before anything is
drawn.

## Intake, in one message
Ask only what the request leaves open. Give your recommended answer and a one-line reason
with every question, so "yes" or "your call" is a complete reply:
- Where it will be posted and who watches.
- The one feeling or takeaway the viewer should leave with.
- Length, and anything that must appear (a character, a product, a message, an ending).
- Tone (funny, warm, bittersweet, epic) and any references they love.

## Concepts
Pitch three different stories, not three versions of one idea. Make one of them bolder than the
others, and say which you recommend and why. For each, give one line of logline, then:
- **Protagonist and want:** who we follow and what they are after.
- **Obstacle:** what stands in the way.
- **Turn:** the surprise or choice that changes things.
- **Ending image:** the final picture, and how it answers the opening.
- **Why it fits:** why this lands for this audience at this length.

## Beat sheet
Develop the chosen concept, or the user's blend of concepts, into a beat sheet timed to the
length. A story spine keeps the arc honest: once there was…, every day…, until one day…,
because of that…, because of that…, until finally…, and ever since…. Each beat is one image
that reads with the sound off, plus what changes in it. At 30 seconds, expect about 6-8 beats
and one small arc, not an epic.

## Director's checks, before you show it
- The protagonist wants something and makes the decisive choice. They are not passive, and luck
  does not rescue them.
- Something is in the way, and the middle escalates. Cut any beat where nothing changes;
  scenery is not a beat.
- There is a turn the viewer doesn't expect but believes.
- The ending pays off the opening: an image or motif returns, changed.
- The payoff reads at phone size in one look. If it needs a close-up, plan the close-up as its
  own beat.
- One specific, surprising detail beats generic charm (a request that hides "one hand, baby's
  asleep").

## Alignment stop
Present the concepts and your recommendation, then the beat sheet for the recommended one, so
the user can approve in a single reply. Wait for approval before the
storyboard or any build. This applies to short pieces too. Silence is not approval. Skip the
stop only when the user supplied a finished story, or explicitly said to go ahead without
reviewing it. Even then, run the checks and name any weakness in one line.

## Evidence
One session (a 32-second cut-paper short, 2026-09-23) had polished animation and a weak story.
The protagonist lost her kite and waited; a 12-second middle was scenery with no obstacle; the
payoff was predictable and only readable in a close-up. The story was never shown to the user
before the build, and the user's verdict was that the animation was good and the story was not.
