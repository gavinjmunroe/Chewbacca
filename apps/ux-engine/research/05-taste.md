# What taste is

Researched 2026-09-20 against primary texts. Every quote below was pulled from
the source document, not from a quote site, except where marked. `[UNVERIFIED]`
means the trail ran out and the claim is repeated rather than checked.
Quoted material inside `>` blocks is reproduced exactly, punctuation included,
so any em dashes below belong to the source and not to this file.

This file exists because `ux-lint` needs a defensible answer to "says who?" The
deny lists in `systems/` are downstream of a 300-year argument about whether
there is anything to deny. Sections 1 to 5 are the argument. Sections 6 to 10
are the part that compiles.

Pair with [research/12](12-developing-taste.md), which covers how taste is
_acquired_ (subtraction neglect, deliberate practice, critique structures).
This file covers what it _is_. Section 7 below is where the two meet: the
expert/novice divergence on prototypicality is the mechanism underneath every
training claim in 12.

## 1. Hume: taste is a sentiment, and sentiments can still be wrong

David Hume, _Of the Standard of Taste_ (1757). Full text at
[davidhume.org](https://davidhume.org/texts/empl1/st). Paragraph and Miller
page numbers below are the ones the source prints.

He concedes the subjectivist case completely, and in stronger terms than most
subjectivists use:

> "Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind
> which contemplates them; and each mind perceives a different beauty. One
> person may even perceive deformity, where another is sensible of beauty; and
> every individual ought to acquiesce in his own sentiment, without pretending
> to regulate those of others." (ST 7)

Then he refuses the conclusion:

> "Whoever would assert an equality of genius and elegance between Ogilby and
> Milton, or Bunyan and Addison, would be thought to defend no less an
> extravagance, than if he had maintained a mole-hill to be as high as
> Teneriffe, or a pond as extensive as the ocean. Though there may be found
> persons, who give the preference to the former authors; **no one pays
> attention to such a taste**; and we pronounce without scruple the sentiment
> of these pretended critics to be absurd and ridiculous." (ST 8, Mil 230-1)

The rules of art are not derived, they are observed:

> "It is evident that none of the rules of composition are fixed by reasonings
> a priori... Their foundation is the same with that of all the practical
> sciences, **experience**; nor are they any thing but general observations,
> concerning what has been universally found to please in all countries and in
> all ages." (ST 9, Mil 231-2)

### The five qualifications, exact

The summary sentence, which is the one to encode:

> "**Strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice,
> perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice**, can alone entitle
> critics to this valuable character; and the joint verdict of such, wherever
> they are to be found, is the true standard of taste and beauty." (ST 23, Mil 241)

Each one, in Hume's own definition:

| Qualification              | Hume's definition                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Delicacy**               | "Where the organs are so fine, as to allow nothing to escape them; and at the same time so exact as to perceive every ingredient in the composition: This we call delicacy of taste." (ST 16)                                                                                                                    |
| **Practice**               | "nothing tends further to encrease and improve this talent, than practice in a particular art, and the frequent survey or contemplation of a particular species of beauty." (ST 18, Mil 237)                                                                                                                     |
| **Comparison**             | "By comparison alone we fix the epithets of praise or blame, and learn how to assign the due degree of each... A man, who has had no opportunity of comparing the different kinds of beauty, is indeed totally unqualified to pronounce an opinion with regard to any object presented to him." (ST 20, Mil 238) |
| **Freedom from prejudice** | "considering myself as a man in general, forget, if possible, my individual being and my peculiar circumstances." (ST 21, Mil 239-40)                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Good sense**             | "reason, if not an essential part of taste, is at least requisite to the operations of this latter faculty... Every work of art has also a certain end or purpose, for which it is calculated; and is to be deemed more or less perfect, as it is more or less fitted to attain this end." (ST 22, Mil 240-1)    |

The Sancho Panza story is the load-bearing analogy, and it is more precise than
its reputation. Two kinsmen taste a hogshead. One reports leather, one reports
iron. Both are ridiculed.

> "On emptying the hogshead, there was found at the bottom, an old key with a
> leathern thong tied to it." (ST 15, Mil 235-6)

The point people miss: Hume says the key did not make them right. It made them
_provable_.

> "Though the hogshead had never been emptied, the taste of the one was still
> equally delicate, and that of the other equally dull and languid: But it
> would have been more difficult to have proved the superiority of the former,
> to the conviction of every by-stander." (ST 16)

### The circularity and how he answers it

He raises the objection himself:

> "But where are such critics to be found? By what marks are they to be known?
> How distinguish them from pretenders? These questions are embarrassing; and
> seem to throw us back into the same uncertainty, from which, during the
> course of this essay, we have endeavoured to extricate ourselves." (ST 24,
> Mil 241)

His answer is a category move, not a proof:

> "But if we consider the matter aright, **these are questions of fact, not of
> sentiment**." (ST 25, Mil 242)

Whether a given person has delicate organs, practice, comparison, and freedom
from prejudice is an empirical question about that person. It is answerable the
way any disputed empirical question is answerable, and badly, but answerably.

The [SEP entry on Hume's aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-aesthetics/)
notes scholars treat this as a regress rather than a strict circle, following
Kivy (1967): identifying a sufficiently delicate critic itself requires
evaluative judgment.

His backstop is duration, not consensus at a moment:

> "The same Homer, who pleased at Athens and Rome two thousand years ago, is
> still admired at Paris and at London. All the changes of climate, government,
> religion, and language, have not been able to obscure his glory. Authority or
> prejudice may give a temporary vogue to a bad poet or orator; but his
> reputation will never be durable or general." (ST 11, Mil 233)

And he fences off two kinds of disagreement that are _not_ errors:

> "there still remain two sources of variation... The one is **the different
> humours of particular men**; the other, **the particular manners and opinions
> of our age and country**." (ST 28, Mil 243-4)

**Falsifiable out of Hume:** a taste claim is defeasible. If a preference
survives (a) delicacy, (b) practice, (c) comparison against the full field,
(d) removal of the judge's personal stake, and (e) a check that the artifact's
own purpose is served, it stands. If it fails any one, it is overridable, and
naming which one it failed is the whole argument. **A generator that cannot say
which of the five a choice fails has not made an aesthetic argument.**

## 2. Kant: the judgment is subjective and still demands your agreement

Immanuel Kant, _Critique of Judgement_ (1790), Bernard translation,
[Gutenberg #48433](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/48433/pg48433.txt).
The four Moments each close with a one-sentence definition. Verbatim:

1. **Quality / disinterestedness.** "_Taste_ is the faculty of judging of an
   object or a method of representing it by an _entirely disinterested_
   satisfaction or dissatisfaction. The object of such satisfaction is called
   _beautiful_."
2. **Quantity / universality.** "The _beautiful_ is that which pleases
   universally, without a concept."
3. **Relation / purposiveness.** "_Beauty_ is the form of the _purposiveness_
   of an object, so far as this is perceived in it _without any representation
   of a purpose_."
4. **Modality / necessity.** "The _beautiful_ is that which without any concept
   is cognised as the object of a _necessary_ satisfaction."

"Purposiveness without purpose" is the third one and it is routinely
mistranslated in design writing as "form without function." Kant's claim is
narrower: the object looks _as if_ it were organised toward an end, and you
register that organisation without needing to know or apply the end.

The mechanism is the free play of the faculties: imagination and understanding
harmonise without the understanding constraining the imagination
([SEP, Kant's Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-aesthetics/)).

### The part that should stop a rule-writer cold

> "**There can be no objective rule of taste which shall determine by means of
> concepts what is beautiful.** For every judgement from this source is
> aesthetical; _i.e._ the feeling of the subject, and not a concept of the
> Object, is its determining ground. To seek for a principle of taste which
> shall furnish, by means of definite concepts, a universal criterion of the
> beautiful, is fruitless trouble; because what is sought is impossible and
> self-contradictory." (§17)

He does leave one empirical door open in the same paragraph:

> "The universal communicability of sensation (satisfaction or dissatisfaction)
> without the aid of a concept--the agreement, as far as is possible, of all
> times and peoples as regards this feeling in the representation of certain
> objects--this is the empirical criterion, **although weak and hardly sufficing
> for probability**." (§17)

And on imitation, directly relevant to a machine trained on examples:

> "Not that taste can be acquired by imitating others; for it must be an
> original faculty. He who imitates a model shows, no doubt, in so far as he
> attains to it, skill; but only shows taste in so far as he can judge of this
> model itself." (§17)

_Sensus communis_ (§20) is what licenses the demand for agreement: a
"subjective principle which determines what pleases or displeases only by
feeling and not by concepts, but yet with universal validity."

The antinomy (§56), verbatim:

> "(1) _Thesis._ The judgement of taste is not based upon concepts; for
> otherwise it would admit of controversy (would be determinable by proofs).
> (2) _Antithesis._ The judgement of taste is based on concepts; for otherwise,
> despite its diversity, we could not quarrel about it (we could not claim for
> our judgement the necessary assent of others)."

His resolution (§57): the Thesis means _determinate_ concepts, the Antithesis
means an _indeterminate_ one. "Between these two there is no contradiction."

**Applicable out of Kant, and it cuts against this whole repo:** every rule in
`ux-lint` is, on Kant's account, a rule about _dependent_ beauty
(_pulchritudo adhaerens_): beauty judged relative to what the thing is for.
That is fine. It is just not a rule of taste in his sense, and it should not be
sold as one. **A linter enforces fitness-to-purpose and consistency. It cannot
enforce beauty, and claiming otherwise is the error Kant names in §17.**

## 3. Bourdieu: the uncomfortable one

Pierre Bourdieu, _Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste_
(French 1979, Nice translation 1984). Quotes pulled from the
[Monoskop scan](https://monoskop.org/images/e/e0/Pierre_Bourdieu_Distinction_A_Social_Critique_of_the_Judgement_of_Taste_1984.pdf);
page numbers are the printed ones. OCR artifacts silently corrected.

He is not making a vague point about culture. He ran a survey and the survey is
the argument. From Appendix 1: the study was carried out in **1963 on 692
subjects in Paris, Lille and a small provincial town**, extended in **1967-68 to
a total of 1,217 subjects**, supplemented with INSEE national data (one cited
sample of 38,000 individuals; a 1972 household-expenditure survey of 13,000
households).

The opening claim, on p. 1, reads:

> "Whereas the ideology of charisma regards taste in legitimate culture as a
> gift of nature, **scientific observation shows that cultural needs are the
> product of upbringing and education**: surveys establish that all cultural
> practices (museum visits, concert-going, reading etc.), and preferences in
> literature, painting or music, are **closely linked to educational level**
> (measured by qualifications or length of schooling) and secondarily to social
> origin."

The famous line, p. 6, with the sentence that follows it (which is the part
that actually does the work):

> "**Taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier.** Social subjects,
> classified by their classifications, distinguish themselves by the
> distinctions they make, between the beautiful and the ugly, the distinguished
> and the vulgar, in which their position in the objective classifications is
> expressed or betrayed."

The mechanism, p. 5-6: the "pure gaze" is not a faculty, it is a position.

> "the detachment of the pure gaze cannot be dissociated from a general
> disposition towards the world which is the paradoxical product of
> conditioning by negative economic necessities — a life of ease — that tends
> to induce an active distance from necessity."

The class split runs form against function:

> "the stylization of life, that is, **the primacy of forms over function**, of
> manner over matter"

...against "the popular disposition which annexes aesthetics to ethics," and
against "the taste of necessity, which favours the most 'filling' and most
economical foods."

The verdict, p. 7:

> "**The denial of lower, coarse, vulgar, venal, servile** — in a word, natural
> — enjoyment, which constitutes the sacred sphere of culture, implies an
> affirmation of the superiority of those who can be satisfied with the
> sublimated, refined, disinterested, gratuitous, distinguished pleasures
> forever closed to the profane. That is why **art and cultural consumption are
> predisposed, consciously and deliberately or not, to fulfil a social function
> of legitimating social differences**."

### He goes after Kant by name

The postscript is titled "Towards a 'Vulgar' Critique of 'Pure' Critiques"
(pp. 485-500). Its thesis, on p. 488, is this:

> "**Kant's principle of pure taste is nothing other than a refusal, a disgust**
> — a disgust for objects which impose enjoyment and a disgust for the crude,
> vulgar taste which revels in this imposed enjoyment."

And on where the doctrine came from, p. 492: Kant's opposition between "free
art" and "mercenary art" "very directly expresses Kant's conception of the
position of 'pure' or 'autonomous' intellectuals in the division of labour."
Disinterestedness is the occupational ideology of a class that can afford to be
disinterested.

**Falsifiable out of Bourdieu, and this one is a real test:** if taste tracks
quality, a preference should predict the artifact's performance. If taste tracks
position, a preference should predict the _judge's_ education and class origin
better than it predicts anything about the artifact. Bourdieu's data said the
second, with education as the dominant variable. **The test for any taste rule
in this repo: does the rule cite an effect on the user, or only on the
evaluator?** A rule whose only defence is "it looks cheap" is a class marker
with a stylesheet.

## 4. Where philosophy currently stands

From [SEP, Aesthetic Judgment](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aesthetic-judgment/)
and [SEP, The Concept of the Aesthetic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aesthetic-concept/):

- The framing question is still Kant's: "what must be the nature of pleasure in
  beauty if the judgments we base on it can make claim to correctness?"
- The consensus position is that aesthetic judgments _are_ normative, even among
  people who deny it in theory. SEP's blunt version: anti-normativists "do not
  practice what they preach."
- Kant's _explanation_ is widely rejected. SEP: "Kant's explanation does not
  work." What survives is his description of the phenomenon, not his account of
  it.
- **Frank Sibley** is the live position for anyone building a rule engine.
  Aesthetic concepts are _not condition-governed_: "we just see (or fail to see)
  that things are delicate, balanced, and the like" (Sibley 2001, 13-14). There
  is no set of non-aesthetic conditions that entails an aesthetic property. His
  critics include Dickie, Cohen, and Kivy.
- **George Dickie (1964)** demolished the "aesthetic attitude" as a distinct
  mode of attention: "there is only one way to listen to (to attend to) music"
  regardless of motive.
- **Monroe Beardsley's** trinitarian thesis (unity, intensity, complexity as the
  three grounds of aesthetic excellence) is the most rule-shaped account on
  offer and SEP calls it implausible.

From [SEP, 18th Century British Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aesthetics-18th-british/),
the original working definition, Addison, _Spectator_ no. 409: taste is "that
faculty of soul, which discerns the beauties of an author with pleasure, and the
imperfections with dislike."

**Applicable:** Sibley is the hard constraint on this project. If aesthetic
properties are not condition-governed, then no finite rule set entails
"balanced." What a rule set _can_ do is enumerate defeaters: conditions under
which the property reliably fails. **Write the deny list, not the definition.**
That is the same conclusion `04-constraints-and-tells.md` reached from the
opposite direction, and it is the strongest single result in this file.

## 5. Design theory

### 5.1 Dieter Rams, ten principles

Verbatim from [Vitsœ](https://www.vitsoe.com/us/about/good-design), which
publishes them as Rams's own text. Written in the late 1970s after Rams asked
himself "is my design good design?"

1. **Good design is innovative.** "The possibilities for innovation are not, by
   any means, exhausted... innovative design always develops in tandem with
   innovative technology, and can never be an end in itself."
2. **Good design makes a product useful.** "A product is bought to be used. It
   has to satisfy certain criteria, not only functional, but also psychological
   and aesthetic. Good design emphasizes the usefulness of a product whilst
   disregarding anything that could possibly detract from it."
3. **Good design is aesthetic.** "The aesthetic quality of a product is integral
   to its usefulness because products we use every day affect our person and our
   well-being. But only well-executed objects can be beautiful."
4. **Good design makes a product understandable.** "It clarifies the product's
   structure. Better still, it can make the product talk. At best, it is
   self-explanatory."
5. **Good design is unobtrusive.** "Products fulfilling a purpose are like
   tools. They are neither decorative objects nor works of art. Their design
   should therefore be both neutral and restrained, **to leave room for the
   user's self-expression**."
6. **Good design is honest.** "It does not make a product more innovative,
   powerful or valuable than it really is. It does not attempt to manipulate the
   consumer with promises that cannot be kept."
7. **Good design is long-lasting.** "It avoids being fashionable and therefore
   never appears antiquated."
8. **Good design is thorough down to the last detail.** "Nothing must be
   arbitrary or left to chance. Care and accuracy in the design process show
   respect towards the user."
9. **Good design is environmentally-friendly.** "It conserves resources and
   minimizes physical and visual pollution throughout the lifecycle of the
   product."
10. **Good design is as little design as possible.** "**Less, but better**
    because it concentrates on the essential aspects, and the products are not
    burdened with non-essentials. Back to purity, back to simplicity."

What #10 actually means, against the common misreading: not _less product_ and
not _less visual weight_. It is less _designing_: fewer authored decisions
layered onto the object. Braun radios are dense with controls. The restraint is
in the absence of gesture, not the absence of content. Rams's own frame is
resource-driven, from his 1976 New York speech _Design by Vitsœ_: "an increasing
and irreversible shortage of natural resources," and "I imagine our current
situation will cause future generations to shudder at the thoughtlessness in the
way in which we today fill our homes, our cities and our landscape with a chaos
of assorted junk."

**Testable:** #6 (honest) and #8 (thorough) are the only two that are
machine-checkable without a human. Honesty is checkable as "does the interface
claim a state it cannot deliver": fake progress bars, skeleton screens over
already-loaded content, disabled buttons with no reason given. Thoroughness is
checkable as coverage: does every state (empty, loading, error, one item, many,
too many, offline) exist. **Ship those two as lint rules. The other eight are
briefs, not rules.**

### 5.2 Massimo Vignelli, _The Vignelli Canon_

Free PDF, quotes from the
[RIT Vignelli Center copy](https://www.rit.edu/vignellicenter/sites/rit.edu.vignellicenter/files/documents/The%20Vignelli%20Canon.pdf).

Structure: **Intangibles** (Semantics, Syntactics, Pragmatics, Discipline,
Appropriateness, Ambiguity, Design is One, Visual Power, Intellectual Elegance,
Timelessness, Responsibility, Equity) then **Tangibles** (paper sizes, grids,
margins, columns and modules, letterhead, book grids, typefaces, alignment, type
size relationships, rulers, contrasting sizes, scale, texture, color, layouts,
sequence, binding, identity, white space).

The order is the argument. Twelve chapters of _why_ before one line about a
grid.

**Semantics** is his first move on every project: "The very first thing that I do
whenever I start a new assignment in any form of design... is to search for the
meaning of it."

**Discipline:**

> "The attention to details requires discipline. There is no room for
> sloppiness, for carelessness, for procrastination. Every detail is important
> because the end result is the sum of all the details involved in the creative
> process no matter what we are doing. **There are no hierarchies when it comes
> to quality. Quality is there or is not there, and if is not there we have lost
> our time.**"

> "**Discipline is a set of self imposed rules, parameters within which we
> operate.** It is a bag of tools that allows us to design in a consistent
> manner from beginning to end... Design without discipline is anarchy, an
> exercise of irresponsibility."

**Appropriateness**, his answer to the style question:

> "appropriateness is the search for the specific of any given problem...
> **Appropriateness transcends any issue of style** - there are many ways of
> solving a problem, many ways of doing, but the relevant thing is that, no
> matter what, the solution must be appropriate."

> "I think that we have to listen to what a thing wants to be, rather then
> contrive it in to an arbitrary confinement."

**Against novelty**, which is the sharpest anti-trend statement in the design
canon:

> "We are definitively against any fashion of design and any design fashion. We
> despise the culture of obsolescence, the culture of waste, the cult of the
> ephemeral. We detest the demand of temporary solutions, **the waste of
> energies and capital for the sake of novelty**."

> "We like a typography that transcends subjectivity and searches for objective
> values, a typography that is beyond times - that doesn't follow trends."

**Design Is One:** "Design is a discipline, a creative process with its own
rules, controlling the consistency of its output toward its objective in the
most direct and expressive way." Not a style.

**Visual Power** vs. impact, the distinction most generators fail:

> "Visual strength is an expression of intellectual elegance and **should never
> be confused with just visual impact** - which, most of the time, is just an
> expression of visual vulgarity and obtrusiveness."

**Typefaces:** he names what he actually uses. Garamond, Bodoni, Century
Expanded, Helvetica: "the aim of the exhibition was to show that a large
variety of printed matter could be done with an economy of type with great
results. In other words, **is not the type but what you do with it that counts.
The accent was on structure rather than type**." Plus Optima, Futura, Univers,
Caslon, Baskerville. About twelve, total, for a career.

**Equity:** when asked to redesign a logo, he counter-proposed a light retouch
for Ford, Ciga Hotels, Cinzano and Lancia. "When a logo has been in the public
domain for more than fifty years it becomes a classic."

**Testable:** Vignelli's whole system reduces to a constraint count. A defensible
project uses roughly **one grid, two typefaces, three type sizes, and a palette
whose hues you can name on one hand**. That is directly checkable against
generated CSS, and it is the sharpest operational rule in this file.

### 5.3 Paul Graham, _Taste for Makers_ (2002)

[paulgraham.com/taste.html](https://paulgraham.com/taste.html). His anti-relativist
argument in one move:

> "If taste is just personal preference, then everyone's is already perfect: you
> like whatever you like, and that's it."

The refutation is that people improve. If your taste in 2026 differs from your
taste in 2016 and you call the change improvement, you have conceded a standard.

His properties of good design, condensed with his own phrasings:
simple; timeless ("If something is ugly, it can't be the best solution. There
must be a better one"); solves the right problem (the stove-dials example, where
"the simplest answer is to put them in a row" is "a simple answer to the wrong
question"); suggestive; often slightly funny; hard; looks easy ("The easy,
conversational tone of good writing comes only on the eighth rewrite"); uses
symmetry (repetition and recursion); resembles nature ("nature has had a long
time to work on the problem"); is redesign ("In Leonardo's drawings there are
often five or six attempts to get a line right"); can copy; is often strange;
happens in chunks ("Being a genetic Leonardo was not enough to compensate for
having been born near Milan instead of Florence"); is often daring.

His formula: "The recipe for great work is: very exacting taste, plus the ability
to gratify it."

From _How to Do Great Work_ (2023),
[paulgraham.com/greatwork.html](https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html):

> "Consciously cultivate your taste in the work done in your field. **Until you
> know which is the best and what makes it so, you don't know what you're aiming
> for.**"

> "Something that still seems good in a hundred years is more likely to be
> genuinely good."

That last one is Hume's test of time restated, 266 years later, apparently
independently.

**Testable:** "Good design is redesign" and "good design looks easy" are the same
claim from two sides and they produce one measurable: **revision count**. A first
draft that shipped is a defect, not efficiency. A generator that emits one pass
and stops has skipped the step both Graham and Hume identify as the source of the
skill.

### 5.4 Rick Rubin, _The Creative Act_ (2023)

Rubin is the clean counter-case to everyone above, and he should be represented
as such, not smoothed into agreement.

His priority order: "In terms of priority, inspiration comes first. You come
next. **The audience comes last.**"

And the flat relativism:

> "Everyone has their own taste. It's not the right taste. It's my taste."

> "You can't second-guess your own taste for what someone else is going to like.
> It won't be good."

Quotes sourced via [readtrung](https://www.readtrung.com/p/steve-jobs-rick-rubin-and-taste),
which pairs them with Rubin's own interviews. `[UNVERIFIED]` against the printed
book, and page numbers are not checked.

**Applicable:** Rubin's position does not translate into a rule and that is the
point. It is a _procedure_, not a criterion: decide from your own response, not a
model of the audience's. Encoded, it means **the generator must have a fixed
point of view it applies before it knows who is looking**. That is what a design
system in `systems/` is. Rubin is the argument for picking one row and staying
in it.

### 5.5 John Maeda, _The Laws of Simplicity_ (MIT Press, 2006)

The ten laws. `[UNVERIFIED]`: these are the standard published formulations,
gathered from secondary listings rather than the book itself, because
lawsofsimplicity.com no longer resolves.

1. **Reduce**: "The simplest way to achieve simplicity is through thoughtful
   reduction."
2. **Organize**: "Organization makes a system of many appear fewer."
3. **Time**: "Savings in time feel like simplicity."
4. **Learn**: "Knowledge makes everything simpler."
5. **Differences**: "Simplicity and complexity need each other."
6. **Context**: "What lies in the periphery of simplicity is definitely not
   peripheral."
7. **Emotion**: "More emotions are better than less."
8. **Trust**: "In simplicity we trust."
9. **Failure**: "Some things can never be made simple."
10. **The One**: "Simplicity is about subtracting the obvious, and adding the
    meaningful."

Law 3 is the only one in the whole design canon that is a latency claim, and it
connects directly to `data/constants.json`. Law 5 and Law 9 are the honest ones:
Maeda's own list contains its defeaters.

### 5.6 The functionalist line and the reaction

Louis Sullivan, _The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered_ (1896). The
actual sentence, which is not the one people quote:

> "Whether it be the sweeping eagle in his flight, or the open apple-blossom,
> the toiling work-horse, the blithe swan, the branching oak, the winding stream
> at its base, the drifting clouds, over all the coursing sun, **form ever
> follows function**, and this is the law. Where function does not change, form
> does not change."

Sullivan's claim is biological analogy, not an engineering rule.

**HfG Ulm (1953-1968)** is where functionalism split. Max Bill, first rector,
founded it explicitly as a Bauhaus continuation (declared in a 1952 prospectus)
and held that "the designer remains an individual creator endowed with talent."
Otl Aicher and Tomás Maldonado pushed a science-and-method model in which "the
designer should be an equal partner in the decision-making process of industrial
design rather than a superior artist." Bill resigned as rector in 1956.
Maldonado replaced him. _Die gute Form_: "a natural product, developed from its
function and technical requirements, that in its form represents its purpose and
is at the same time attractive."

**This is the original version of the argument this repo is having.** Bill's
position is that taste is a talent. Maldonado's is that it is a method. Ulm
resolved it by firing the talent. Rams and Braun are downstream of the Maldonado
side.

The reaction, Robert Venturi, _Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture_
(MoMA, 1966):

> "Where simplicity cannot work, simpleness results. Blatant simplification
> means bland architecture. **Less is a bore.**"

From the "Nonstraightforward Architecture: A Gentle Manifesto" section:

> "I am for **messy vitality over obvious unity**. I am for richness of meaning
> rather than clarity of meaning; for the implicit function as well as the
> explicit function."

**Applicable:** Venturi is the named opposition to every minimal-by-default rule
in this repo, and he beat Ulm in the market for twenty years. The honest version
of the Rams/Vignelli position is not "simplicity is good," it is "**simplicity is
correct where the artifact's purpose is instrumental**." Venturi was arguing
about buildings people live in and walk past. Most interfaces are closer to a
Braun radio than to a Philadelphia house. **Where an interface is expressive
rather than instrumental (a landing page, a launch, a game), the Rams deny list
is the wrong system and `ux-pick` should say so.**

## 6. Contemporary tech discourse

### 6.1 "Taste is the new moat"

The claim is everywhere and the sourcing is thin. What I actually verified:

**Ruben Dominguez**, The VC Corner (April 2026),
[thevccorner.com](https://www.thevccorner.com/p/why-taste-is-the-new-moat). The
best definition in the genre:

> taste is "**repeatable, high-fidelity judgment under uncertainty**"

He explicitly separates it from aesthetics and frames it as judgment about what
deserves to exist. Evidence cited: Microsoft reporting 20-30% of new code
AI-written, Coinbase at 40%, an Axios study finding 40% of desk workers hit
"workslop" costing roughly two hours of rework monthly. He cites Paul Graham and
Rex Woodbury. **He presents no data that taste-driven products outperform
financially.**

**Andrés Max**, [andresmax.com](https://andresmax.com/taste-is-the-new-moat/):
taste is "the cumulative effect of hundreds of small decisions that determine how
something makes you feel." "It's not decoration, it's alignment." His claims:
"Code got cheap. Taste didn't"; taste "can't be copied on a freelance
marketplace"; a three-second recognition test. Cites a Y Combinator figure that
25% of a recent batch had codebases mostly written by AI. `[UNVERIFIED]`: I did
not check that against YC's own statement.

**Brighteye Ventures**,
[brighteyeweekly](https://brighteyeweekly.substack.com/p/taste-is-the-new-ai-moat-b90):
"When everyone can build, building stops being the advantage." "Conviction can't
be copied." No sources cited, no counter-arguments raised.

**Finding, and it is the honest one:** across three pieces from investors and
operators, the argument is identical, the definitions differ, and **none of them
offers evidence that taste predicts commercial outcomes**. The genre asserts a
moat and measures nothing. Dominguez's definition is worth keeping. The thesis is
unsupported as of September 2026.

### 6.2 Jobs and Ive

Steve Jobs, _Triumph of the Nerds_ (PBS, 1995):

> "The only problem with Microsoft is they just have no taste. They have
> absolutely no taste."

Jobs to Mike Moritz, _Fortune_ (1983), which is the more useful one:

> "**I don't think my taste in aesthetics is that much different than a lot of
> other people's. The difference is that I just get to be really stubborn about
> making things as good as we all know they can be.**"

That is a claim that taste is not perception but **enforcement**, and it lines up
with Vignelli's "discipline" exactly.

Jony Ive, sourced via [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Jonathan_Ive)
with dates:

> "I think there is a profound and enduring beauty in simplicity; in clarity, in
> efficiency. **True simplicity is derived from so much more than just the
> absence of clutter and ornamentation. It's about bringing order to
> complexity.**" (WWDC 2013, iOS 7 video)

> "It's sad and frustrating that we are surrounded by products that seem to
> testify to a complete lack of care." (_Daily Telegraph_, November 2005)

> "**Being superficially different is the goal of so many of the products we
> see**... rather than trying to innovate and genuinely taking the time,
> investing the resources and caring enough to try and make something better."
> (_Globe and Mail_, January 2007)

> "I think a beautiful product that doesn't work very well is ugly."
> (_Vanity Fair_, October 2014)

The widely-circulated "Simplicity is not the absence of clutter, that's a
consequence of simplicity" is attributed to Ive across many sites but I could not
find it in a dated primary source. `[UNVERIFIED]`.

Ive's inevitability test, _Icon Magazine_ (July 2003): the target is a solution
where you think "of course it's that way, why would it be any other way?"

**Testable:** the inevitability test is the only one in this section that can run
on an artifact. **For each authored decision, is there a reason it is that value
and not an adjacent one?** A spacing scale with a reason is taste. A spacing
scale whose values could all shift by 2px with no consequence is decoration.

### 6.3 "Why Slop Matters" (2026)

Kommers, Duede, Gordon, Holtzman, McNulty, Stewart, Thomas, So, Long.
[arXiv 2601.06060](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.06060), 13 January 2026. The only
peer-directed paper I found that tries to formalise the thing this repo is
fighting.

They refuse a definition and give family resemblances instead, which is the
Sibley move:

> "AI Slop is not a natural kind: its boundaries will not be formally delineated
> in a way that conclusively separates slop from non-slop."

Three prototypical properties:

1. **Superficial Competence**: "demonstrates a kind of competence that would
   take real expertise or skill to achieve without AI; this is belied by a lack
   of underlying substance, craft, or communicative intent... characterized by
   overtly 'idealized' output, **flattening out individual variation into
   canonical patterns**."
2. **Asymmetric Effort**: "generated with a facile prompt and requires little or
   no effort of the kind that would be necessary to create such an output
   without AI."
3. **Mass Producibility**: "designed to work within a digital ecosystem of
   production and distribution to reach a mass audience."

Three dimensions of variation: instrumental utility, personalization, surrealism.
They cite Bourdieu's _Distinction_ as reference [1].

**Applicable, and this is the most directly useful finding in the file:** their
property #1, "flattening out individual variation into canonical patterns," is
the same mechanism `04-constraints-and-tells.md` identified as the empty deny
list. Two independent lines of work converged on **regression to the median** as
the defining property of generated output, which means the detector should look
for medianness rather than for ugliness.

## 7. The empirical layer

This is where the philosophy stops being unfalsifiable. Everything below is a
published result with a direction.

### 7.1 Processing fluency

Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, "Processing Fluency and Aesthetic Pleasure: Is
Beauty in the Perceiver's Processing Experience?" _Personality and Social
Psychology Review_ 8(4), 2004, 364-382.
[PDF](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf).

The hypothesis, verbatim from the abstract:

> "aesthetic pleasure is a function of the perceiver's processing dynamics: **The
> more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their
> aesthetic response.**"

Four assumptions, verbatim from the overview:

1. "objects differ in the fluency with which they can be processed. Features
   that facilitate fluent processing include all the core features identified in
   the objectivist tradition, like **goodness of form, symmetry, figure-ground
   contrast**, as well as variables that have not received attention in
   traditional theories of aesthetic pleasure, like perceptual and conceptual
   priming procedures."
2. "processing fluency is itself **hedonically marked** and high fluency is
   subjectively experienced as positive, as indicated by psychophysiological
   findings."
3. "processing fluency feeds into judgments of aesthetic appreciation because
   people draw on their subjective experience in making evaluative judgments,
   **unless the informational value of the experience is called into question**."
4. "the impact of fluency is **moderated by expectations and attribution**...
   fluency has a particularly strong impact on affective experience if its source
   is unknown and fluent processing comes as a surprise... the fluency-based
   affective experience is **discounted** as a source of relevant information
   when the perceiver attributes the experience to an irrelevant source."

Fluency-raising variables they review: goodness of form, figure-ground contrast,
symmetry, prototypicality, repeated exposure (mere exposure, Zajonc 1968),
implicit learning of stimulus structure, visual and semantic priming.

Prototypicality results they cite: positive relation between prototypicality and
aesthetic evaluation in colour patches (Martindale & Moore 1988), paintings
(Hekkert & van Wieringen 1990), furniture (Whitfield & Slatter 1979), faces
(Langlois & Roggman 1990; Rhodes & Tremewan 1996).

**The defeater, and it is the whole ballgame for this repo:** Smith & Melara
(1990) found prototypical chord progressions "were preferred by novices **(though
not by experts)**."

On the golden ratio, which the design internet treats as settled: "evidence for
preference of the golden section is mixed (Green, 1995; Kubovy, 2000)" and they
decline to use it. **Do not put 1.618 in `data/constants.json`.**

**Testable, and this is the single most useful mechanism in the file:**
prototypicality raises liking _in novices_ and stops doing so _in experts_. That
is a mechanism for Hume's "practice" and "comparison" and Bourdieu's "cultural
capital" simultaneously, and it explains the generated look exactly. **A model
trained on the median produces maximally prototypical output, which is maximally
fluent, which novices rate highly and practitioners rate as slop.** Both
populations are responding accurately to the same stimulus. Neither is wrong.

### 7.2 Aesthetic-usability effect

Kurosu & Kashimura (1995): **252 participants, 26 ATM interface variations**. The
finding, as [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)
states it: "the correlation between the participants' ratings of aesthetic appeal
and perceived ease of use was stronger than the correlation between their ratings
of aesthetic appeal and actual ease of use." Replicated by Tractinsky in Israel
(1997, 2000) with a different cultural sample. Later support: Sonderegger & Sauer
(2010), Shi, Huo & Hou (2021).

The specific correlation coefficients that circulate for this study
(commonly r = .589 and r = .132) are **`[UNVERIFIED]`**: I could not reach the
primary paper and NN/g does not print them. Do not ship those numbers.

The limit, which matters more than the effect: "A visually appealing design can
forgive **minor** usability problems only: not major ones... When products
suffer from severe usability issues, or when functionality is sacrificed for
aesthetics, users tend to lose patience."

**Testable:** beauty buys forgiveness on small failures and none on large ones.
So the payoff curve for polish is non-monotonic with task difficulty. **Spend the
aesthetic budget on flows that work. On a broken flow it converts to resentment.**

## 8. What they agree on, across 300 years

Six things, and they are agreed on by people who agree on nothing else.

1. **Taste is trained, not innate.** Hume ("practice... comparison"), Bourdieu
   ("the product of upbringing and education"), Graham ("consciously cultivate
   your taste"), Reber et al. (expertise reverses the prototypicality
   preference), Vignelli ("discipline is a set of self imposed rules"). Bourdieu
   and Graham are mortal enemies about what the training _means_ and identical
   about the fact of it.
2. **Comparison across the full field is the mechanism.** Hume is explicit that
   without it a judge is "totally unqualified." Graham: "Until you know which is
   the best and what makes it so, you don't know what you're aiming for."
   Bourdieu's data shows exposure volume is the thing education actually buys.
3. **Duration is the best available test.** Hume's Homer, Kant's "agreement of
   all times and peoples" (offered weakly), Rams #7, Vignelli's timelessness,
   Graham's hundred years, Vignelli's fifty-year logo rule.
4. **Novelty for its own sake is the named failure mode.** Rams #1 ("can never be
   an end in itself"), Vignelli ("the waste of energies and capital for the sake
   of novelty"), Ive ("being superficially different is the goal of so many of
   the products we see"), Kant (imitation shows skill, not taste).
5. **Fitness to purpose is a component, not a rival.** Hume's "every work of art
   has also a certain end or purpose." Rams #2 and #3 ("only well-executed
   objects can be beautiful"). Ive ("a beautiful product that doesn't work very
   well is ugly"). Vignelli's appropriateness. Even Kant handles it, as dependent
   beauty.
6. **The skill shows up as refusal.** Rams #10, Vignelli's discipline, Jobs's
   stubbornness, Graham's "toss everything unnecessary out of your pack,"
   Bourdieu's "Kant's principle of pure taste is nothing other than a refusal."
   Bourdieu means it as an indictment. Everyone else means it as a method. They
   are describing the same behaviour.

## 10. The strongest case against taste

Assembled honestly, not as a straw man. This is the version to beat.

1. **The empirical case.** Bourdieu ran the study. Across 1,217 subjects plus
   national data, aesthetic preference was "closely linked to educational level
   (measured by qualifications or length of schooling) and secondarily to social
   origin." If taste were tracking a property of artifacts, the strongest
   predictor of a judgment should be something about the artifact. It was
   something about the judge.
2. **The mechanism case.** The "pure gaze" requires distance from necessity,
   which requires not being under necessity. Bourdieu, p. 5: the disposition is
   "the paradoxical product of conditioning by negative economic necessities: a
   life of ease." Disinterestedness is not a faculty, it is a budget.
3. **The function case.** On p. 7: art and cultural consumption are "predisposed...
   to fulfil a **social function of legitimating social differences**." This is
   falsifiable and it has not been falsified. When a designer says an interface
   "looks cheap," the word is _cheap_.
4. **The circularity case.** Hume's true judge is identified by having good
   taste; good taste is identified by the verdict of true judges. Hume's escape
   ("questions of fact, not of sentiment") relocates the problem rather than
   dissolving it. SEP records it as an unresolved regress.
5. **The Kantian case against rules.** §17: seeking "a universal criterion of the
   beautiful... is impossible and self-contradictory." Sibley's modern version:
   aesthetic properties are not condition-governed. Every rule engine, this one
   included, is doing something other than what it claims.
6. **The confound case.** Reber et al. give a complete deflationary mechanism:
   liking tracks processing fluency, fluency tracks familiarity and
   prototypicality, and familiarity tracks exposure, which tracks class and
   education. Taste could be _entirely_ exposure history wearing a robe. The one
   piece of evidence against total deflation is that experts stop preferring
   prototypes, which is also exactly what Bourdieu predicts a distinction-seeking
   elite would do.
7. **The gatekeeping case in tech.** Three "taste is the moat" essays, three
   different definitions, zero outcome data. A term with no agreed definition and
   no measurable referent, used to explain who deserves to be hired and funded,
   is doing social work. Bourdieu named this exact move in 1979.

**What survives all seven.** One thing, and only one: **experts and novices
diverge systematically and in a predictable direction** (Smith & Melara 1990,
via Reber et al.). If taste were purely a class marker, expert judgment would be
arbitrary with respect to the artifact and would track only the expert's
position. Instead it tracks a specific artifact property, prototypicality, and
moves against it. That is a real, measurable, direction-having skill, and it is much
smaller than what "taste" is usually asked to mean.

## 11. The operational definition

The shortest true statement I can defend from the above:

> **Taste is trained discrimination: the ability, acquired by high-volume
> comparison within a domain, to detect which of two near-identical artifacts is
> worse, and to name the specific feature responsible.**

Four load-bearing pieces, each traceable:

- **Trained**: Hume's practice and comparison; Bourdieu's education variable;
  the expert/novice divergence in Reber et al. Nobody across 300 years claims
  it is innate.
- **Discrimination**: Hume's delicacy: "organs so fine, as to allow nothing to
  escape them." It is a resolution claim, not a preference claim. Two people can
  prefer differently and only one can be discriminating.
- **Near-identical**: where the skill actually lives. Anyone can rank a masterpiece
  above a disaster. The measurable difference is on adjacent options, which is
  also why it is invisible from outside and reads as gatekeeping.
- **Name the feature**: Sancho's kinsmen were right before the key was found and
  could not prove it. Hume says the general rules of composition exist so you can
  "silence the bad critic." **An unarticulated preference is a preference. An
  articulated one is a judgment.** This is the clause that separates taste from
  class signalling, because it is the clause that can be checked.

What the definition deliberately excludes: any claim that taste detects beauty,
that it is universal, that it predicts commercial outcomes, or that the trained
judge is a better person. None of those survive section 10.

## 13. What does not survive translation into a rule

Stated plainly so nobody tries.

- **Kant's beauty.** §17 forbids it explicitly and he is right. What a linter
  enforces is dependent beauty: fitness to a concept of what the thing is. Do not
  label that column "beauty."
- **Sibley's aesthetic properties.** "Balanced," "elegant," "graceful" are not
  entailed by any set of non-aesthetic conditions. A generator can produce
  defeaters for them, never definitions.
- **"Intellectual elegance."** Vignelli's central value, and he defines it by
  ostension: Greek statues, Renaissance painting, Goethe. Unencodable as written.
- **Rubin's self-trust.** Not a criterion. The encodable residue is procedural:
  commit to one point of view before knowing the audience. That is what picking
  one row of `SYSTEMS.md` already does.
- **"Taste is the new moat."** Three definitions, no outcome data, September 2026.
  Keep Dominguez's phrasing, "repeatable, high-fidelity judgment under
  uncertainty", and discard the moat claim until somebody measures it.
