## Executive Summary

This document defines Tizzy's core user personas and their journeys from awareness to mastery. Understanding these users—their pain points, goals, and daily contexts—is critical for building a product that genuinely helps, not just "checks feature boxes."

**Primary Users:**
1. **Alex (College Student, 19):** Autistic young adult navigating new social environment
2. **Maya (Parent, 42):** Mother of 12-year-old autistic son, seeking tools to help him succeed
3. **Dr. Sarah Chen (SLP, 35):** Speech-language pathologist specializing in autism

**Secondary Users:**
4. **Jordan (High School Student, 16):** Autistic teen struggling with peer relationships
5. **Ms. Rodriguez (Special Ed Teacher, 48):** Educator supporting autistic students in transition

## Persona 1: Alex (Autistic College Student)

### Demographics
- **Age:** 19
- **Location:** Boston, MA (attending college)
- **Diagnosis:** Diagnosed with autism at age 8, high support needs for social communication
- **Living Situation:** Dorm room, first time away from home
- **Tech Proficiency:** High (uses smartphone, laptop, Discord, TikTok daily)

### Background & Context

Alex was diagnosed with autism in elementary school. They received speech therapy for years, focusing on conversation skills, topic maintenance, and understanding social cues. In high school, Alex mostly talked to a small friend group who understood their communication style. College is different: new people, fast-paced social expectations, group projects, dorm conversations, and constant sensory overload.

Alex knows they struggle in conversations but can't always pinpoint what went wrong in the moment. They often replay conversations in their head later, feeling frustrated or embarrassed. Their parents want to help but can't observe what's happening at school. Alex wants to improve but doesn't know *how*.

---

### Pain Points

1. **"I know I messed up, but I don't know what I did wrong."**
   - Conversations break down, but Alex can't identify the specific moment or behavior that caused it.
   - Therapists ask "What happened?" but Alex can't describe it accurately.

2. **Sensory overload makes conversations collapse.**
   - Loud cafeteria, crowded dorm lounge, multiple people talking = shutdown.
   - By the time Alex realizes they're overwhelmed, it's too late.

3. **Masking is exhausting, but not masking leads to social rejection.**
   - Alex tries to "act normal" (eye contact, fake laughter, mirroring others) but it's draining.
   - When they stop masking, peers say they're "weird" or "rude."

4. **Therapy doesn't generalize.**
   - Alex did well in clinic sessions (structured, predictable, one-on-one).
   - Real-world conversations (fast, chaotic, group dynamics) are completely different.

5. **Parents can't see what's happening.**
   - Weekly phone calls: "How's it going?" "Fine." (But it's not fine.)
   - Parents worry but have no visibility into actual struggles.

---

### Goals

**Immediate (Month 1-3):**
- Understand *why* conversations break down (specific patterns, not vague "social skills")
- Get real-time feedback that helps in the moment (not 24 hours later)
- Reduce anxiety about social interactions

**Medium-Term (Month 3-6):**
- Improve 1-2 specific skills (e.g., knowing when to exit a conversation, asking return questions)
- Feel more confident in group settings (study groups, dorm hangouts)
- Reduce masking burnout (communicate authentically without constant rejection)

**Long-Term (6-12 months):**
- Build 2-3 close friendships (people who "get" them)
- Stop replaying conversations obsessively
- Enjoy college social life instead of dreading it

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done

**Functional:**
- "Help me see what I can't see in my own conversations."
- "Tell me when I'm about to mess up, so I can course-correct."
- "Show me progress so I know this is working."

**Emotional:**
- "Make me feel less broken."
- "Help me communicate as myself, not a fake version I think people want."
- "Reduce the anxiety of not knowing what went wrong."

**Social:**
- "Help me make friends without constant social crashes."
- "Give my parents visibility so they stop worrying (but don't helicopter)."

### User Journey: Alex's First 90 Days with Tizzy

#### Week 0 (Pre-Purchase): Awareness & Discovery

**Trigger:** Alex's mom reads about Tizzy on an autism parent forum. She texts Alex: "Have you heard of this? Might help with conversations."

**Alex's Reaction:**
- Skeptical ("Another app that promises magic and delivers nothing")
- Curious (wearable = less intrusive than pulling out phone)
- Worried about privacy ("Will people know I'm recording them?")

**Touchpoints:**
- Visits Tizzy website, watches demo video
- Reads reviews from other autistic college students (Reddit, TikTok)
- Sees therapist endorsement (from a SLP Alex respects online)

**Decision:** Willing to try 30-day free trial ("If it sucks, I'll just return it").

---

#### Week 1: Onboarding & Setup

**Day 1: Device Arrives**
- Unboxes Tizzy necklace (sleek, doesn't scream "medical device")
- Charges it, downloads app, pairs via Bluetooth
- Watches 3-minute setup video (how to wear it, privacy controls, haptic meanings)

**Day 2-3: First Wear**
- Wears it to class (nervous, but no one notices)
- Feels first haptic pulse during conversation with roommate (double pulse = "ask a return question")
- Doesn't act on it (too distracted), but feels it

**Day 4-7: Post-Conversation Reviews**
- Opens app after cafeteria conversation (group of 4 people, felt awkward)
- Sees timeline: "You talked for 2 minutes straight without pausing. They looked confused."
- Insight: "Try asking a question after 30 seconds of talking."
- Micro-goal: "Tomorrow, ask one return question in a conversation."

**Emotional State:** Hopeful but cautious. "This is interesting. Let's see if it actually helps."

---

#### Week 2-4: Early Behavior Change

**Pattern Recognition:**
- App shows recurring issue: "You interrupt others mid-sentence 6+ times per day."
- Alex didn't realize this was happening (thought they were just enthusiastic)
- Haptic pulse (single) now triggers awareness: "Wait, let them finish."

**Small Win (Week 3):**
- Study group conversation: Alex pauses, lets classmate finish, then responds.
- Classmate smiles, says "Good point."
- Post-conversation review: "Great job! You waited for 3 pauses today."

**Emotional State:** Encouraged. "Holy shit, this might actually work."

---

#### Week 5-8: Skill Improvement & Confidence Growth

**Coaching Focus:** Topic maintenance
- App detects Alex shifts topics abruptly ("You changed subject 4 times in 5 minutes").
- New micro-goal: "Stay on topic for 2 full turns before shifting."
- Haptic long pulse when topic shift is too abrupt = cue to reconnect

**Social Win (Week 7):**
- Coffee chat with potential friend from class.
- Tizzy buzzes (escalation warning): Alex is talking too intensely about special interest.
- Alex catches self, asks: "Sorry, am I nerding out too hard? What do you think?"
- Friend laughs: "No, it's cool! I didn't know you were into this."
- Conversation continues smoothly.

**Emotional State:** Confident. "I'm not broken. I just needed to *see* what I was doing."

---

#### Week 9-12: Mastery & Reduced Dependence

**Behavior Internalization:**
- Alex now catches interruptions *before* Tizzy buzzes.
- Post-conversation reviews become validation ("Yep, I thought I did better—stats confirm it").
- Haptic feedback used less frequently (only in high-stress/new situations).

**Long-Term Progress:**
- Friendships forming (2 people Alex hangs out with regularly)
- Group project goes smoothly (no awkward silences or interruptions)
- Parents notice: "You sound happier on calls."

**Tizzy's Role:** Shifts from "coach" to "safety net." Alex still wears it but relies on it less.

**Emotional State:** Grateful. "This changed how I see myself."

---

### Key Metrics for Alex Persona

**Product Engagement:**
- Daily wear rate: 6-7 days/week

**Outcome Metrics:**

## Persona 2: Maya (Parent of Autistic Child)

### Demographics
- **Age:** 42
- **Location:** Austin, TX
- **Family:** Married, two kids (Ethan, 12, autistic; Sophie, 9, neurotypical)
- **Occupation:** Marketing manager (works from home 3 days/week)
- **Tech Proficiency:** Medium (uses smartphone, Zoom, basic apps)

### Background & Context

Maya's son Ethan was diagnosed with autism at age 5. He's been in speech therapy since kindergarten, focusing on conversation skills and social reciprocity. Ethan is verbal, academically strong, but struggles with friendships. Middle school has been brutal: he's been excluded from lunch groups, misunderstood jokes, and had meltdowns after school because of social overload.

Maya feels helpless. She can't be at school to see what's happening. Ethan can't articulate what went wrong ("I don't know, everyone just left"). Teachers say "He's doing fine academically" but miss the social struggles. Maya has tried social skills apps, but Ethan loses interest after a week.

---

### Pain Points

1. **"I can't see what's happening at school."**
   - Ethan says conversations are "fine," but Maya knows they're not.
   - Teachers don't observe lunchtime or recess (where most social struggles happen).

2. **"Therapy doesn't transfer to real life."**
   - Ethan does great in clinic sessions (structured, predictable).
   - School conversations are chaotic, fast-paced, and full of implied meaning he misses.

3. **"I don't know how to help."**
   - Maya isn't a therapist. She tries to coach Ethan but feels like she's guessing.
   - "Did you ask them questions?" "Did you wait your turn?" → Generic advice that doesn't address specific patterns.

4. **"Apps don't work."**
   - Social skills apps are gamified, but Ethan gets bored fast.
   - No real-world feedback loop = no behavior change.

5. **"I worry about his future."**
   - Middle school is hard. High school will be worse.
   - Will Ethan have friends? Will he be able to navigate college? Get a job?

---

### Goals

**Immediate (Month 1-3):**
- Understand what's actually happening in Ethan's conversations (specific patterns, not vague "social skills")
- Help Ethan improve 1-2 skills (e.g., asking return questions, exiting conversations gracefully)
- Reduce after-school meltdowns (less social overload)

**Medium-Term (Month 3-6):**
- See measurable progress (fewer "no one wants to talk to me" reports)
- Collaborate with therapist using real-world data (better IEP goals)
- Ethan makes at least 1 friend

**Long-Term (6-12 months):**
- Ethan feels more confident socially (less masking, less burnout)
- Maya feels less helpless (has tools to support him)
- Positive ripple effects (better school experience, improved mental health)

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done

**Functional:**
- "Show me what's happening in Ethan's conversations so I can help."
- "Give Ethan real-time coaching that actually works (not another app he ignores)."
- "Provide data I can share with his therapist and school."

**Emotional:**
- "Make me feel like I'm not failing as a parent."
- "Give me hope that Ethan will be okay."
- "Reduce my constant worry about his social life."

**Social:**
- "Help Ethan make friends so he's not lonely."
- "Give his teachers visibility so they take his struggles seriously."

## Persona 3: Dr. Sarah Chen (Speech-Language Pathologist)

### Demographics
- **Age:** 35
- **Location:** Seattle, WA
- **Occupation:** SLP at private pediatric therapy clinic (specializes in autism)
- **Caseload:** 25 autistic clients (ages 8-18), 15-20 hours/week direct therapy
- **Tech Proficiency:** Medium-high (uses iPad for assessments, teletherapy platforms)

### Background & Context

Dr. Chen has been practicing for 10 years, focusing on social communication and pragmatic language for autistic clients. She's passionate about her work but frustrated by the limits of clinic-based therapy. Her clients do well in structured 1-on-1 sessions but struggle to generalize skills to school, home, and peer interactions.

Parents often ask: "Why is my child doing well in therapy but still struggling at school?" Dr. Chen knows the answer (clinic ≠ real world), but she has no way to see what's happening outside her office. She relies on parent reports (often vague) and school observations (inconsistent). She wishes she could extend her impact beyond the weekly 1-hour session.

---

### Pain Points

1. **"I can't see real-world conversations."**
   - Therapy happens in clinic: structured, predictable, low sensory load.
   - Real world is chaotic: cafeteria noise, group dynamics, unpredictable topics.
   - Dr. Chen has no visibility into what actually happens.

2. **"Generalization is the hardest part."**
   - Clients learn skills in clinic but don't use them in real life.
   - Parents report: "He does it with you, but not at school."
   - Dr. Chen can't troubleshoot if she doesn't see the breakdown.

3. **"Parent reports are unreliable."**
   - Parents mean well but can't describe patterns accurately.
   - "He had a bad day" → What does that mean? What specific behaviors?

4. **"I'm limited to 1 hour/week."**
   - 1 hour of therapy can't compete with 40+ hours of school, peers, home.
   - Dr. Chen wishes she could provide ongoing coaching, but she can't be there 24/7.

5. **"Outcome tracking is guesswork."**
   - Pre/post assessments (e.g., ASHA benchmarks) happen every 6 months.
   - Between assessments, it's subjective: "I think he's improving?"

---

### Goals

**Immediate (Month 1-3):**
- See real-world conversational patterns for 3-5 pilot clients
- Validate whether Tizzy data aligns with clinic observations
- Determine if Tizzy can improve therapy effectiveness

**Medium-Term (Month 3-6):**
- Use Tizzy data to set more specific IEP goals (not vague "improve social skills")
- Reduce time spent on guesswork ("What happened at school?")
- Improve client outcomes (measurable progress on ASHA benchmarks)

**Long-Term (6-12 months):**
- Integrate Tizzy into standard practice for autism caseload
- Publish case study or pilot research (professional credibility)
- Differentiate clinic from competitors ("We use cutting-edge assistive tech")

---

### Jobs-to-Be-Done

**Functional:**

**Emotional:**

## Cross-Persona Insights

### Common Themes

1. **"I know something's wrong, but I don't know what."**
   - All users share this frustration (autistic users, parents, therapists, teachers)
   - Tizzy's value = making the invisible visible

2. **Generalization gap (clinic ≠ real world)**
   - Therapy works in structured settings, fails in chaotic real life
   - Tizzy bridges this gap with real-world coaching

3. **Privacy is non-negotiable**
   - Every persona worries about recording, surveillance, bystander consent
   - Tizzy must be radically transparent (privacy dashboard, clear controls)

4. **Measurable outcomes build trust**
   - Users need proof it works (not vibes, but data)
   - Weekly progress reports, specific metrics (interruptions down 50%, etc.)

5. **Community matters**
   - Autistic users want peer validation (Reddit, TikTok reviews)
   - Parents want testimonials from other autism parents
   - Therapists want peer community (SLP Facebook groups, conferences)

## Conclusion: Build for Humans, Not Features

These personas are not fictional. They represent real pain, real needs, and real opportunities to help.

**Product decisions should always ask:**
- How does this help Alex feel less anxious?
- How does this give Maya visibility into Ethan's struggles?
- How does this make Dr. Chen a better therapist?

**If a feature doesn't serve a persona's core job-to-be-done, it's distraction, not value.**

Build for these people, and Tizzy becomes essential assistive technology. Ignore them, and Tizzy becomes another failed ed-tech gadget.
