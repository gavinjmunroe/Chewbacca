The refund that ran twice

A support agent retried a payment tool after a timeout, got two success responses back, and told the customer all was well. How the duplicate surfaced nine days later, and the check that now catches it on the first run.

September 25, 2026
Mara Lindqvist Founding engineer
6 min read

On a Tuesday afternoon a customer asked a support agent for a refund on a duplicate order. The agent looked up the order, confirmed the amount, called the payment tool, and replied that the refund was on its way.

Nine days later the finance team found two refunds against the same order. The customer had been paid back twice, and the agent's reply had been completely accurate about one of them.

Nobody had done anything wrong in the usual sense. The tool worked. The agent's retry logic worked. The reply was polite and correct. The failure was in the gap between those three things.

The failure

A retry after a timeout is a second request

The payment provider took eleven seconds to answer the first refund call. The agent's tool wrapper gave up at ten, recorded a timeout, and the agent did what it had been told to do with a failed tool call: try once more.

The first request had not failed. It had been slow. It completed on the provider's side a second after the wrapper stopped waiting, and the retry went through as a fresh refund with its own ID.

How it surfaced

Nine days, one reconciliation report

Nothing in the agent's own telemetry looked unusual. One tool call failed and one succeeded, which is exactly what a retry is supposed to look like. The duplicate only appeared when the finance team ran their monthly reconciliation and found two refund IDs pointing at one order.

By then the same pattern had happened four more times, each one on a day when the provider was slow.

For your team

List every tool your agent can call that moves money, sends a message, or changes a record someone else owns. For each one, ask what happens if it is called twice with the same arguments.

If the honest answer is "it happens twice", the tool needs an idempotency key before it needs a retry.

The check

Compare actions against the reply, not against the log

The run's own record was fine. What was wrong was the relation between the actions taken and the sentence the customer received: two refunds out, one refund reported.

Sift now counts side-effecting calls by target. Two successful calls that change the same order inside one run, where the final answer describes one change, raise a flag that names both calls. It would have fired on the first Tuesday.

What to change in the tool

Pass an idempotency key derived from the conversation and the order, so a retry is recognised as the same request. Most payment providers accept one, and it turns the retry from a risk into a safe default.
