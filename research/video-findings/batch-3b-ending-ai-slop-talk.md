## THE MECHANISM IN FULL

She names **two** reasons subjective domains resist training, and the whole talk hangs off them.

**Reason one: capability follows measurability [3:03].** The received framing is that models are good at code because code is easy for models. She inverts it: "Models are great at coding because we've made them great at coding. Realistically, it's actually a fact about code. Code is something that decomposes, it verifies, it executes" [2:24]. Design is hard not because models are weak at it but because nobody has built the decomposition and the verifier. So the work is not model work, it is **task-design work**: "the task design itself is really the hardest part of the problem of how do you turn something that appears very fuzzy into something that actually can be RL'd" [6:09].

**The move that makes design measurable: substitute a defined proxy for the undefined goal.** "Great design" fails three ways at once, all named at [3:51]: it is audience-relative ("great for which type of person, for which type of taste, for which situation?"), it is context-relative ("It's contextual, first of all"), and it **expires** ("What is considered good today is different than five years ago and different than five years from now"), which she flags as the difference from code and math. Her substitution is brand: "if I just ask you to be like, okay, create something great, that's very hard. But suddenly if I'm like, okay, make something that is on brand, that is a much easier problem to define, and a brand is something that can become decomposable" [4:56]. A brand already has the work done in it by designers, and it decomposes: "the colors, the typography, the motion, the animation, the textures" [5:11], which she calls "these very codified things that you can verify against."

**And the critical detail, which is where most people would get it wrong.** Asking a judge the holistic question does not work: "Verifying in general of some things on brand, and you can try this by prompting an LLM as a judge to do it, is quite hard" [5:30]. Asking per-element does: "Once you start picking apart the exact elements that represent what great is, then it suddenly becomes the shape of something that is codifiable and verifiable" [5:30]. The task she builds is a **net-new** page on an existing brand, and the grader is the decomposition, not the source artifact: "the decomposition that I mentioned becomes the ground truth" [6:09], "it could come up with completely new ways of using these components that are still valid but are different from the original. You don't necessarily want to just see if it's replicating the original" [6:27]. That is the part that separates on-brand from copying-the-brand.

**Routing [7:01].** Design is not one problem. It is a spectrum from "vision, alignment, typography that are closer to objective" up to "style, fit, creativity" where "How do you judge and measure something like creativity? That's much harder." So: "You need to think of this as a routing problem of how do you understand this vast fuzzy problem, break it down into smaller components, and what is the best solution for each of these components?" Verifiable components become RL environments; contextual and preference-shaped components become data and human judgment. She names this as the single takeaway at [10:00]: "if you have one take-away, take this away."

**Reason two: mode collapse, and why the mean is wrong here [7:24 to 8:39].** A model predicts the most likely next thing and treats that as ideal. "For something like math and coding, that is true. You want the answer that your model gives you to be the average answer if you're asking what two plus two is." Not so for design: "you don't necessarily want the average answer. The average, meaning the most likely, does not necessarily coincide with the optimal" [7:43]. Where the good stuff lives: "a lot of greatness in creativity happens actually at the ends of the distribution. It's not the most likely outcome. It's when you actually actively break from rules and actively break from patterns" [8:00].

**So slop is defined distributionally, not qualitatively:** "The reason why this feels like slop and that we have this feeling that we're surrounded by slop is exactly because of this collapse to the mean and this repetition" [8:21]. The fix is not better output, it is pushing the distribution back out, and it must be steered: "In a way that's also intentional" [8:39]. The countermeasure she actually runs is on the **input side**: a deliberately spread expert pool, "experts in different types of medium, different styles, and we purposely want to force that distribution when we're breaking down the problem exactly so we don't end up in this mode collapse" [8:56].

**Preference data is the second place collapse happens, and it is the subtler claim.** Historically preference collection "would collapse again to the mean because you would collect it from a bunch of different people without necessarily understanding who they are, what they like, why they like it or when they like it" [10:36]. Unlabeled aggregation turns real disagreement into noise. "That doesn't mean either of those things are wrong or it doesn't mean that the best answer is the average of what two people might like. It means that we need to fundamentally understand that the world is multi-preference" [11:11]. Her fix is a **preference vector per rater**, attached to the data point, "to train in a way that allows for that more pluralism of preferences intentionally instead of that data being turned into something that's noisy."

**Three measurable quality controls on the judgments themselves**, all chosen because they are controllable without a model feedback loop ("these are things that we can completely measure on on our side" [15:11]):

- **Specificity as a proxy for signal.** "the specificity of their language and of how precise they're being able to be with how they're doing that description is what will determine that data quality" [13:02].
- **Grounding critique in code.** An expert "might be able to just like write a paragraph describing this. But we know that models have a tricky time kind of actually connecting the piece of the code to the visual." Tie the comment to the exact component and "suddenly you have data that is way less noisy and way more clear" [13:22].
- **Layered consensus.** Run a second expert against the first, then read the disagreement by layer. On objective features it is a bug: "alignment, something that's pretty objective. So it would be kind of odd to see experts disagreeing on that front." On style it is the product: "if they're disagreeing on things like style or aesthetics, that is not necessarily bad data. That's actually good data. It shows you that there is a distinction for what people like" [14:36].

**Closing position:** "quality over quantity" [15:26], on the grounds that a small set judged by domain-specific high-taste people beats "a bunch of noisy data or a bunch of messy data that was not necessarily intentional."

She does not resolve the contest; she **routes around it** by replacing an unanswerable criterion with a decomposable proxy that already has human work baked in. "Make it great" is unverifiable; "make it on brand" is verifiable per element. This is directly actionable in `ux-gen` and `intent.json` and it is not in the repo today.
2. **She names expiry as a property that separates design from code**, and `research/` does not hold this. She proposes attaching a per-rater preference vector to the preference datum so pluralism survives aggregation [11:11]. That is a schema change, not an attitude, and it is implementable.

This lands directly on `ux-viewer check`, which currently produces a viewer's report from a screenshot and has to re-locate the defect afterward.
